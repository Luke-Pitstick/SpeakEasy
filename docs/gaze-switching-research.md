# Gaze-only workspace switching: research notes

Research date: October 23, 2026. This is ideation, not an accepted implementation specification or a claim that a design has been validated with SpeakEasy users.

The UI draft was paused when the user redirected the work to research. Existing working-tree changes remain outside this research note; no application validation conclusions follow from this document.

## Primary evidence

1. **Off-screen activation can conserve the workspace, but is not established as an accessible default.** Isokoski's [Text Input Methods for Eye Trackers Using Off-Screen Targets (ETRA 2000)](https://doi.org/10.1145/355017.355020) proposes off-screen target sequences to separate command gestures from ordinary looking and conserve display area. It explicitly identifies target number and location as usability issues. This is an experimental text-entry system, not evidence that an arbitrary upward glance safely changes an AAC mode. The abstract does not establish ALS participant validation. For SpeakEasy, a broad, visible on-screen edge control is a more conservative hypothesis than requiring successful tracking beyond the display.

2. **Gestures need feedback and cannot be assumed faster than dwell.** Heikkilä and Räihä's [Speed and Accuracy of Gaze Gestures (2009/2010)](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/2290) reports six gesture tasks: execution was slower than expected, curved shapes were difficult, and path accuracy was sometimes poor. Authors suggest more feedback. The study concerns drawing-like gestures rather than a simple mode-switch gesture, so it cautions against complicated gesture vocabularies without ruling out a single broad edge movement. It does not establish ALS usability.

3. **Blink speed is not equivalent to better usability.** Roig-Maimó, Mas-Sansó and MacKenzie's [standardized comparison of blink, wink and dwell (ETRA 2026)](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/etra2026a.html) used 12 university volunteers with no eye-tracking experience. Blink throughput was 2.11 bits/s, dwell 1.95, and wink 1.56; dwell had the lowest error rate (1.82%), while wink errors reached 20.4%. Ten of twelve preferred dwell; nine called wink most tiring. The experiment used a fixed 600 ms dwell and controlled target-acquisition tasks. It does not determine an appropriate SpeakEasy dwell duration or demonstrate results in people with ALS. Blink should be an optional individually evaluated shortcut, not a required navigation skill.

4. **Prediction's correction cost can erase its benefit.** Pannasch et al.'s [Eye Typing in Application: A Comparison of Two Systems with ALS Patients (2008)](https://www.mdpi.com/1995-8692/2/4/25) compared EyeGaze with hierarchical, predictive GazeTalk in four people with ALS; one withdrew halfway through. EyeGaze performed better on most criteria. Authors note that wrongly selected predicted words had to be removed character by character. This small, confounded comparison of two entire systems does not establish that prediction or dynamic interfaces are generally inferior. It does justify testing the whole recovery path, including selecting a word, spelling its replacement, undoing it, and returning to phrase selection.

5. **Useful adaptation can happen without relocating controls.** Mott et al.'s [Improving Dwell-Based Gaze Typing with Dynamic, Cascading Dwell Times (CHI 2017)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/improving-dwell-based-gaze-typing-dynamic-cascading-dwell-times/) changes required dwell duration according to key likelihood and position. Seventeen non-disabled participants averaged 12.39 WPM with cascading dwell versus 10.62 with static dwell. A small five-person ALS evaluation averaged 9.51 WPM using cascading dwell. This supports investigating adaptation of interaction timing while retaining target locations; it does not prove adaptive workspace switching, and the ALS result should not be presented as the same controlled comparison.

## Related evidence checked by the coordinating agent

- The [SpeakFaster study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53873-3) describes automatic abbreviation-expansion calls alongside cognitive costs from composing initials, reviewing suggestions and deciding the next action. Automatic generation does not establish that automatic screen switching helps. Its eye-gaze ALS evaluation involved two users, limiting generalization.
- Tobii Dynavox's [TD Snap accidental-selection guidance](https://us.tobiidynavox.com/blogs/support-articles/how-to-prevent-accidental-selections-with-eye-gaze-in-td-snap-1) describes requiring the user to look away before selecting again. Its [TD Snap user manual](https://download.mytobiidynavox.com/Snap/documents/TD_Snap_UsersManual_v1-21_en-US_12005874_WEB.pdf) documents delay after page change and an off-screen pause capability specific to TD Pilot. These are concrete product precedents for transition protection, not controlled evidence for a universal gesture or threshold.

## Candidate design hypotheses

- **Visible edge reveal and deliberate activation.** Reserve a broad edge zone outside Dasher's steering region. Entering it pauses steering and reveals a large, stable “Phrase choices” action; deliberate dwell activates it. The panel stays open until explicitly left. Merely looking back at the draft or away from the screen never closes it. An always-visible cue makes the control discoverable; its hit area must be large enough without consuming a full tab row. This is an inference from the evidence, not a published validated design.
- **A fixed contextual action as the comparison condition.** One large action in the same predictable region reads “Phrase choices” during entry and “Back to spelling” during choice. It removes the tab row while retaining an explicit destination. Compare it with edge reveal rather than assuming the extra reveal step is worthwhile. A changed label/action requires fresh gaze activation, so a retained fixation cannot immediately undo the switch.
- **Dasher-native phrase branch as an exploratory condition.** A dedicated branch might reuse continuous steering to request choices. It needs engine-specific evaluation: reachability, discoverability, accidental branch entry, and whether the branch interferes with exact characters or reversal. Existing gesture evidence does not validate this option.

## Constraints and evaluation

Keep the draft in one compact, persistent location. Avoid duplicating accepted text and the same edit text in multiple header rows; retain only information that identifies the active operation. Preserve readable text and large interaction targets rather than shrinking everything to reduce chrome.

Pause steering before opening choices; discard dwell on mode, target, geometry or content changes. Require leaving the switch target before it can activate again. Keep a return path present during loading, errors and empty results. Tracking loss must cancel activation, not count as a blink or off-screen command. Word repair should preserve the rest of the phrase and offer clear commit, cancel and undo operations.

Compare explicit fixed action versus edge reveal with intended users and actual eye trackers. Measure accidental switches during reading/thinking, intentional-switch time, recovery time, selected-word correction completion, and fatigue/preferences over repeated use. Test edge reliability at each person's usable gaze range. Do not choose a universal dwell threshold or infer accessibility from mouse/synthetic gaze success. Screen-space savings are useful only if the new activation and recovery costs stay acceptable.
